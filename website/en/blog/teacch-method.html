The TEACCH method: what does the research say?

When supporting children with an autism spectrum disorder (ASD), I use the TEACCH method as one of the building blocks. Not because it's the newest trend, but because it has been studied for decades.

A 2018 review study, published in the academic journal Papeles del Psicólogo, brought together the findings of fourteen separate studies on TEACCH. Here's what that research found, and what it means for the guidance offered at CederStam.

What is TEACCH again?

TEACCH stands for "Treatment and Education of Autistic and related Communication Handicapped Children." It's a structuring approach that makes time, space and tasks visual and predictable for children with autism: through clear daily schedules, a consistent layout of the work area, and step-by-step instructions using visual aids. The goal isn't to make a child "fit in," but to create an environment in which they can function more independently, calmly, and with more confidence.

What did the study look at?

Researchers Sanz-Cervera, Fernández-Andrés, Pastor-Cerezuela and Tárraga-Mínguez brought together fourteen studies, covering 590 participants aged 2 to 41, published between 2007 and 2017. They looked both at the children's own development and at the impact on stress levels reported by parents and teachers.

The key findings

For the children: improvement was found across all fourteen studies, and in eleven of them that improvement was statistically significant. The gains included language, imitation, perception, motor skills and social interaction, along with fewer autism traits and less problem behaviour.

For parents and teachers: of the seven studies that measured stress, five found a significant decrease. Parents reported a greater sense of competence and control in interacting with their child.

One notable finding: children who started TEACCH before 40 months of age (roughly 3.5 years old) made more progress on average than children who started later.

TEACCH isn't a fad: it's an approach with solid international evidence behind it, though the researchers themselves stress that no single method works identically for every child.

What this means for the guidance at CederStam

These findings confirm what I also see in practice: TEACCH works, but not as a fixed formula applied the same way to every child. That's why I always combine the structure of TEACCH with other techniques, tailored to the pace, talents and needs of the child in front of me. As with everything at CederStam: no single approach stands apart from the child it's meant for.

Source: Sanz-Cervera, P., Fernández-Andrés, M.ª I., Pastor-Cerezuela, G., & Tárraga-Mínguez, R. (2018). Efectividad de las intervenciones basadas en metodología TEACCH en el trastorno del espectro autista: un estudio de revisión. Papeles del Psicólogo, 39(1), 40-50.
